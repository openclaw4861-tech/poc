Pacific Glazing Corporation October 31, 2026

Executive Summary

AI agents have moved from cloud infrastructure to field-ready hardware—the runtime now fits in a technician's pocket, using accessibility APIs as the actuation layer, with no API gating or metering possible. Simultaneously, security threats have emerged across distribution, binary, behavior, and endpoint layers in a single week, signaling systemic rather than isolated risk. The economic center of gravity is shifting: generation cost collapses toward zero while verification and acceptance become the scarce resources, with "AI-verified installation" emerging as a differentiation claim.

Top 10 Technology Trends

1. On-Device AI Agents (Hardware-Accessible)

What it is: AI agent runtimes that execute entirely on mobile devices (dsh-Go via DeepSeek harness, Mika via Android accessibility APIs), using the device's own interfaces as the actuation layer.

Why it matters: Infrastructure dependencies disappear. Field workflow automation—inspection, documentation, CRM updates—becomes technically feasible on devices technicians already carry. Offline capability is built-in, not bolted on.

2. Agent Security: Systemic Trust Chain Decomposition

What it is: Simultaneous threats across four layers identified in one week:

  • Distribution: Backdoored Claude Code extensions on Cursor Marketplace
  • Binary: BLA toolkit poisons executables against AI reverse-engineering
  • Behavior: "Pre-Crime" detection for agent-to-agent recruitment pressure
  • Endpoint: Zero-Breach-Toolkit for mercenary-spyware forensics

Why it matters: Defenses only get built against mature threats. AI-assisted binary analysis has already commodified. Assume proprietary software, installation procedures, and internal tooling are readable by commodity AI tooling today.

3. Verification Infrastructure (Watermarking as Baseline)

What it is: TextSeal2 watermarking survives speculative decoding—provenance defense now operates below the inference optimization layer. The $2B+ market for verification infrastructure is validated by Typesafe AI's $870M raise.

Why it matters: Post-installation documentation and client reports should adopt watermarking proactively. "AI-verified installation" becomes a differentiation claim, not just a compliance posture. Provenance is becoming a procurement requirement.

4. The Cleanup Economy

What it is: Post-generation economics formalizing as a distinct market. Examples:

  • Cisco's unlimited-token rewrite mandate
  • "Unslop" (de-AI-ing UI)
  • Ub-mode (standards enforcement as installable)

Why it matters: When generation is free, acceptance becomes the bottleneck. Expect "verified output acceptance rate" to replace lines-of-code as the enterprise productivity KPI. Verification relocates from vendor level to employee level.

5. Phone-as-Control-Plane Architecture

What it is: The agent runtime itself moves to the pocket—not mobile clients for cloud agents. Two independent implementations (dsh-Go, Mika) converge on accessibility APIs as the universal automation substrate, bypassing API-based access controls entirely.

Why it matters: This is a structural shift in how automation deploys. The control plane follows the device, not the cloud.

6. Skills as Distribution Units

What it is: Agent skills (video generation, genealogy, landing pages, UI cleanup) standardizing across Claude Code, Codex, and OpenClaw—a de facto app store nobody announced. Four of fifteen trending GitHub repos are agent skills.

Why it matters: Installation procedure knowledge becomes an installable skill—potential distribution channel or vulnerability to competitors. Skills function simultaneously as expertise productization and top-of-funnel marketing.

7. Consumer LiDAR Production-Ready

What it is: Consumer-grade LiDAR hardware has crossed from prototype to production-ready, removing the last technical barrier for automated site surveys.

Why it matters: Spatial measurement is now a smartphone capability. Site surveys that previously required specialized equipment can be captured with devices already in the field.

8. Visual Foundation Models for Spatial Intelligence

What it is: Models capable of tracking occluded objects through visual inference, complementing hardware-based spatial sensing.

Why it matters: Software capability is catching up to hardware availability. Near-term deployment relies on LiDAR hardware; 12-18 month planning should account for model-capability improvements that reduce hardware dependency.

9. Provenance Requirements in Enterprise Procurement

What it is: Distribution-path verification becoming a line item in enterprise procurement policies. Skills and plugins require provenance documentation before deployment.

Why it matters: Establish skill/plugin provenance as a procurement requirement now. This is transitioning from nice-to-have to baseline expectation.

10. Productivity Metrics Shift

What it is: Traditional metrics (lines-of-code, task completion) being replaced by "verified output acceptance rate" as the measure of AI productivity.

Why it matters: Refinement and acceptance workflows become the scarce bottleneck. Internal processes and employee evaluation criteria need to align with this emerging standard.

Trend Overlaps

On-Device Agents + Phone-as-Control-Plane + Consumer LiDAR These three trends form the technical foundation for field automation: the runtime moves to the device, uses device interfaces for actuation, and leverages device hardware for spatial capture. Together, they enable inspection-to-documentation workflows without cloud dependency.

Security + Provenance + Skills Distribution The security threat landscape creates demand for provenance verification, which in turn requires procurement policies for skills and plugins. These form a regulatory-response cluster: threats drive requirements, requirements drive procurement standards.

Cleanup Economy + Verification Infrastructure + Productivity Metrics The economic shift toward acceptance as the scarce resource creates demand for verification tooling, which creates new metrics. These form an economic-feedback loop: generation abundance drives refinement scarcity, which drives verification need, which drives metric evolution.

Skills Distribution + Installation Procedure Knowledge Skills-as-distribution creates both opportunity and vulnerability: PGC's proprietary installation knowledge becomes a portable, distributable asset that could be captured by competitors or licensed as a service offering.

MVP Experiments

Experiment 1: AI Binary Security Audit

Time: 1 day Cost: Minimal (existing tools + staff time) Setup:

  1. Use a commodity AI tool (e.g., Cursor with Claude, or a local model) to analyze one proprietary script or installation procedure document
  2. Document what the AI extracts, summarizes, or replicates
  3. Test one marketplace extension by running it in an isolated environment and monitoring behavior

Success criteria: Clear picture of what your proprietary knowledge looks like to AI tooling. Baseline for security posture.

Experiment 2: Smartphone Site Survey Capture

Time: 2-3 days Cost: Device procurement (if needed) + staff time Setup:

  1. Acquire a LiDAR-equipped smartphone (iPhone Pro series or equivalent Android)
  2. Capture measurements of one completed installation using the device's native LiDAR sensor
  3. Export the spatial data and attempt to generate a standardized site report from it
  4. Compare accuracy and time-to-report against current methods

Success criteria: Determine whether consumer LiDAR accuracy meets PGC's documentation standards. Identify gaps between capture and usable output.

Experiment 3: Documentation Watermarking Trial

Time: 1-2 days Cost: Minimal (evaluation of tooling) Setup:

  1. Select one client report or installation documentation generated with AI assistance
  2. Apply TextSeal2 or equivalent watermarking to the output
  3. Test whether the watermark survives copy-paste and format conversion
  4. Document the workflow required to apply watermarking consistently

Success criteria: Understanding of watermarking workflow overhead. Assessment of whether current output quality justifies proactive provenance claims.

Strategic Implications for PGC

Field Workflow Automation On-device AI agents eliminate infrastructure barriers for field automation. Inspection, documentation, and CRM updates could flow automatically from technician interactions, reducing administrative overhead and improving response time.

Security-First Architecture Requirement The simultaneous emergence of threats across distribution, binary, behavior, and endpoint layers means security cannot be an afterthought. Any AI tooling, agent platform, or third-party skill must be audited before deployment. Assume your proprietary software and procedures are readable by commodity AI today.

Competitive Differentiation Through Verification "AI-verified installation" is an emerging differentiation claim. PGC can lead by adopting watermarking proactively—positioning accuracy and output attestation as a service quality feature rather than a compliance requirement.

Installation Knowledge as Asset or Liability Skills-as-distribution means your proprietary installation knowledge could be captured, packaged, and distributed by others. Treat this as a strategic asset requiring protection—or consider licensing it as a service offering before competitors do.

Site Survey Automation Is Feasible Now Consumer LiDAR has removed the hardware barrier for automated site surveys. The remaining gap is software integration—converting raw spatial capture into standardized reports. This is a solvable problem with current technology.

Procurement Policy Update Needed Skill and plugin provenance should become a procurement requirement. Distribution-path verification is becoming a line item in enterprise purchasing. PGC should establish this standard before it becomes mandatory.

Acceptance Rate as Emerging KPI Watch for enterprise procurement to shift from measuring AI output volume to measuring AI output acceptance. Internal processes should begin tracking verification and refinement overhead to prepare for this transition.

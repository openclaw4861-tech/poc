Tiny Experiments

How to Live Freely in a Goal-Obsessed World

By Anne-Laure Le Cunff

Core Premise

Traditional goal-setting is broken. We set SMART goals, make five-year plans, and expect a straight line from effort to fulfillment. But life doesn't work that way. Le Cunff — a neuroscientist, former Googler, and founder of Ness Labs — argues that fixed goals breed anxiety, burnout, and the "arrival fallacy" (the belief that happiness awaits at the destination, not the journey). Her alternative: treat your life as a laboratory, your ambitions as hypotheses, and each step forward as a tiny experiment.

Key Concepts

1. From Linear Goals to Circular Growth

The traditional model: decide your destination → plot the most direct path → execute with willpower and discipline. The flaws:

  • Assumes you already know what you want (you don't — desires emerge through exploration)
  • Frames failure as avoidable (creates anxiety, not motivation)
  • Encourages rigidity (when circumstances change, you can't adapt)

The alternative: circular growth, where goals are discovered, pursued, and adapted — not in a vacuum, but in conversation with the larger world.

2. The Arrival Fallacy

We tell ourselves: "Once I get the promotion / buy the house / hit my revenue target, I'll finally be happy." But the happiness rarely arrives — and when it does, it's fleeting. This is the arrival fallacy: the mistaken belief that happiness lives at the destination rather than in the journey.

Le Cunff's solution: detach your sense of fulfillment from specific outcomes. Find satisfaction in the learning, not the result.

3. Response 1 vs. Response 2

When faced with uncertainty, you have two possible responses:

  • Response 1: Automatic, anxiety-based reaction — you cling to rigid goals for safety, or avoid action altogether
  • Response 2: Autonomous, agency-based engagement — you acknowledge uncertainty and move forward anyway, treating it as information to learn from

The shift from Response 1 to Response 2 is the core psychological muscle the book trains.

4. What Is a Tiny Experiment?

A tiny experiment is a small-scale, purposeful commitment designed to help you explore possibilities with minimal risk. Key features:

  • Low stakes: If it "fails," you lose little — but you gain insight
  • Time-boxed: Specific start and end dates (days to weeks, not months to years)
  • Hypothesis-driven: "I wonder what would happen if I..."
  • Data-focused: The goal isn't success — it's learning

Example: Instead of "I'm going to launch a full construction software review business," try "I'll write three review posts this week and see which gets traction."

5. The Pact

A tiny experiment needs structure. Le Cunff introduces the concept of a pact — a formal agreement you make with yourself:

  • What are you curious about? (the hypothesis)
  • What exactly will you do? (the experiment design)
  • How long will you run it? (the timeline)
  • How will you measure learning? (the data collection)
  • What will you do after? (the reflection phase)

The pact creates accountability without the pressure of traditional commitments.

6. Procrastination as a Compass

Stop treating procrastination as an enemy. Le Cunff suggests asking: Why am I avoiding this?

  • Fear of failure? Downsize the experiment until the stakes feel manageable
  • Not actually interested? Useful data — this path isn't right for you
  • Overwhelmed by scale? Break it into even tinier experiments

Procrastination becomes a signal, not a flaw.

7. Learning in Public

Share what you're experimenting with as you go:

  • You get real-time feedback from people who've done similar things
  • You build an audience interested in your journey
  • You overcome the fear of being judged — because you've already exposed the process

Le Cunff emphasizes that learning in public doesn't mean broadcasting every failure. It means documenting your process honestly, so others can learn from it too.

8. The In-Between as Opportunity

Life's transitions — between jobs, after a project ends, during slow seasons — are often viewed as anxiety-inducing voids. Le Cunff reframes them: these are the ideal times for tiny experiments. You have slack capacity, fewer constraints, and maximum optionality. Use the "in-between" to test new directions without the pressure of full commitment.

Practical Applications for PGC

AI takeoff automation

The tiny experiment mindset is exactly how you've been approaching this — testing small pieces (spec sifter, digital counter, voice-to-scope) instead of trying to build the whole system at once. Formalize it: write a one-paragraph "pact" for each experiment before you start.

New software adoption

Instead of committing to a full platform migration, run a 2-week tiny experiment: have one crew test the new tool on one project. Measure what you learn, not whether it "won." Decision becomes obvious without the pressure.

Business development

Curious about a new market segment (retrofit work? high-end residential?) Run a tiny experiment: visit one potential client, prepare one targeted proposal. Gather data, don't judge outcome.

Leadership & culture

Encourage your team to run tiny experiments on their own workflows. Give them permission to "fail" as long as they learn. Create a simple shared doc where people post: "I tried X and learned Y." This is how you build an experimental culture.

One-Sentence Summary

Stop chasing fixed goals — treat your life as a laboratory where small, low-stakes experiments teach you what actually fulfills you, and let your direction emerge from what you learn instead of forcing it.

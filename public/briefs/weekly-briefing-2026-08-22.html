Pacific Glazing Corporation August 22, 2026 Prepared for: Steve Watts, CEO

Executive Summary

The most significant development this week: edge AI has crossed from pilot promise into production reality, removing the cloud dependency barrier that has stalled on-site deployment for years. Simultaneously, the industry's competitive pivot has shifted from "what can AI do" to "how do we know it did it"—governance and auditability are now the primary enterprise selling points, not raw capability. For PGC, this creates a narrow 90-day window to establish proprietary knowledge capture and audit infrastructure before competitors lock in their positions. The liability model for autonomous agents remains unresolved, which means PGC must build its own internal frameworks before field deployment.

Top 10 Technology Trends

1. Edge AI Production Readiness

What it is: Inference hardware and software stacks that run AI models directly on local devices—no cloud connection required. Recent benchmarks show FPGA-based inference achieving 0.56 tokens per second with bit-exact accuracy against reference models.

Why it matters: On-site deployment is no longer constrained by connectivity or latency. PGC can deploy AI-assisted measurement, compliance checking, or estimator tools in the field without depending on cellular or Wi-Fi access. Cloud dependency is no longer a valid excuse for inaction.

2. Skill Extraction Pipelines

What it is: Automated systems that capture expert workflows through passive observation, induce task-specific models from traces, and package results as installable artifacts. Tools like Codex, Claude Code, and Grok offer early marketplaces for these packaged skills.

Why it matters: The tacit knowledge held by PGC's senior glaziers—site assessment heuristics, unusual material handling tricks, customer negotiation patterns—becomes a scalable, deployable asset. Capture it now or lose it to turnover and retirement.

3. Agent Governance as Differentiator

What it is: Accountability tooling built into AI agents—audit trails, signed execution records, "decide before, record after" decision logging. Vendors are productizing governance features rather than waiting for regulatory mandates.

Why it matters: For traditional industries like glazing, the question is always "how do we know it did it correctly?" Governance tooling answers that question. PGC should position "provable AI" as the selling point, not "smarter AI."

4. Domain Composability

What it is: Modular AI skills that stack across related domains—glazing expertise combined with safety compliance, energy codes, project management, and permitting workflows. Composability enables workflow packages that competitors cannot replicate quickly.

Why it matters: PGC's defensible moat is not AI model quality—models commoditize fast. The moat is the proprietary integration layer combining glazing skills with adjacent compliance and project knowledge. This is where PGC wins long-term.

5. Provenance Infrastructure Instability

What it is: Tools that strip C2PA metadata and statistical watermarks from AI-generated content are gaining rapid adoption (930+ GitHub stars). Regulatory mandates for AI content labeling are being actively undermined by accessible removal tooling.

Why it matters: PGC should not build workflows dependent on content labeling as a trust anchor. The provenance mandate wave will fail as an enforcement mechanism. Invest in internal audit systems instead of external labeling compliance.

6. Agent Routing Standards

What it is: Infrastructure layer that routes agent requests to appropriate skill modules, tools, or models—analogous to DNS for the early internet. Emerging standards like MCP (Model Context Protocol) indicate growing adoption.

Why it matters: Skills built today must be compatible with tomorrow's routing infrastructure. PGC's skill library needs routing-compatible architecture from the start, or re-platforming costs will accumulate. Monitor MCP adoption closely.

7. Liability Framework Gaps

What it is: The legal and insurance framework for autonomous agent actions does not exist. When an agent with a signed purchase mandate acts fraudulently or causes damage, no established liability model assigns responsibility.

Why it matters: This gap will stall enterprise adoption broadly. PGC must establish its own internal liability framework—decision authority boundaries, escalation protocols, human-in-the-loop checkpoints—before deploying agents in any field operation with real consequences.

8. Capability-Realization Optimization

What it is: The gap between what an AI model can theoretically do and what the agent harness actually extracts. Raw model quality matters less than delivery infrastructure: prompt engineering, routing logic, tool integration quality.

Why it matters: PGC should invest in harness quality alongside domain expertise. Better prompt design and routing logic yield more improvement than switching to a "better" foundation model. The bottleneck is now orchestration, not foundation models.

9. Knowledge Capture as Competitive Moat

What it is: Systematic documentation and extraction of institutional knowledge—installation procedures, customer objection handling, material specification expertise—into structured, deployable digital assets.

Why it matters: Knowledge capture is PGC's highest-priority immediate action. Without content, skill extraction pipelines have nothing to work with. Senior employees carry years of irreplaceable context. Capture it now while they are still employed.

10. Audit-Ready AI Systems

What it is: Architecture decisions that make every AI action traceable, reproducible, and verifiable: immutable logging, decision-point documentation, evidence attachment to recommendations.

Why it matters: Enterprise customers and project stakeholders will increasingly demand audit trails. Building auditability into PGC's AI systems from day one avoids costly retrofitting. This also supports any future regulatory compliance requirements.

Trend Overlaps

Governance + Liability = Enterprise Entry Requirement

The intersection of agent governance tooling (Trend 3) and liability framework gaps (Trend 7) creates a paradox: the tools exist to make AI accountable, but no one knows who holds liability when the accountable agent acts. For PGC, this means governance implementation must precede or accompany deployment, and internal liability rules must be explicit before agents make consequential decisions.

Routing + Composability = Infrastructure Moat Timing

Agent routing standards (Trend 6) and domain composability (Trend 4) combine to define where infrastructure moats form. Early standardization on routing-compatible architecture locks in skill library value. Late adoption means rebuilding. The 90-day window exists partly because routing infrastructure is still malleable—standardize now, own the integration layer.

Capability-Realization + Knowledge Capture = Dominant Lever

The capability-realization optimization insight (Trend 8) means that harness quality determines outcome quality—but harnesses process content. Without knowledge capture (Trend 9), even perfect orchestration yields nothing. Together, these trends identify the dominant investment: capture glazing expertise first, then optimize the delivery infrastructure.

Provenance Instability + Audit-Ready = Internal Trust Architecture

The Provenance Infrastructure Instability finding (Trend 5) eliminates external labeling as a trust mechanism. This forces PGC to build internal trust architecture—audit trails, decision logging, evidence attachment. Audit-ready systems (Trend 10) become not just a competitive advantage but a basic requirement for operating AI in regulated or liability-exposed contexts.

MVP Experiments

PGC can validate AI infrastructure assumptions in 1-7 days with minimal investment.

Experiment 1: Document Processor Infrastructure Test

Time: 2-3 days Cost: Under $500 (API calls + internal staff time) Setup:

  • Collect 50 PDFs of existing PGC documents: specification sheets, installation manuals, safety checklists, past project quotes
  • Deploy an open-source document parsing tool (e.g., LlamaParse or similar) against a subset
  • Test extraction accuracy for structured data: material specs, measurements, compliance codes
  • Log infrastructure requirements: latency, failure modes, integration points

Success criteria: Extracts 80%+ of key data fields correctly from 40+ of 50 documents Value: Validates document processing pipeline, identifies data quality issues, establishes infrastructure baseline for future skill building

Experiment 2: Knowledge Inventory Sprint

Time: 3-5 days Cost: Staff time only (no external tools required) Setup:

  • Interview three senior glaziers using structured protocol: decision scenarios, exception handling, customer communication patterns
  • Document findings in structured format: if-then rules, common objections, site assessment criteria
  • Map interview outputs to the five MVP experiments from the strategic roadmap
  • Identify which knowledge is documented vs. tacit

Success criteria: Identifies 20+ distinct tacit knowledge items not currently in documentation Value: Quantifies knowledge capture opportunity, prioritizes extraction targets, provides content inventory for Experiment 1 pipeline

Experiment 3: Agent Accountability Evaluation

Time: 1-2 days Cost: Under $200 (staff time + documentation review) Setup:

  • Map current PGC decision points in the estimation and ordering workflow
  • For each decision point, document: who approves, what data is required, what could go wrong
  • Evaluate two governance frameworks (e.g., OpenBot's "decide before, record after" model and signed audit trail patterns)
  • Identify which framework maps to PGC's liability tolerance

Success criteria: Produces a decision-point map with governance recommendations for each node Value: Establishes internal liability framework, identifies where human-in-the-loop is non-negotiable, provides requirements for agent deployment

Strategic Implications for PGC

1. The 90-day window is real, and it is closing. Edge AI has removed the technical barrier. Governance tooling exists. The remaining barriers are organizational: knowledge capture, liability framework, and routing-compatible architecture. Competitors are not moving faster, but the window for first-mover advantage in glazing-specific AI integration is narrow. Prioritize the three critical actions now.

2. Skills are the moat, not models. Foundation models commoditize. Proprietary glazing knowledge combined with adjacent compliance and project management expertise does not. PGC should allocate resources to knowledge capture and skill packaging, not to model selection or training.

3. "Provable AI" outperforms "powerful AI" for this market. PGC's customers—general contractors, project managers, building owners—want documented, auditable decisions. They do not want black-box recommendations. Position AI as accountability infrastructure, not automation.

4. Liability frameworks must precede deployment. The liability cliff is not theoretical. PGC needs explicit internal rules before any agent makes a decision with financial or safety consequences. This is a legal and operational requirement, not a technical one.

5. Infrastructure compatibility is a long-term cost lever. Skills built today on non-routing-compatible architecture will require re-platforming. Monitor MCP adoption and industry routing standards. Build modular, standards-aware from the start.

6. Capability-realization gap means focus on orchestration, not models. PGC's technical team should prioritize prompt engineering, tool integration quality, and routing logic over foundation model evaluation. The same model produces dramatically different results depending on harness quality.
